Take a breather.

Your spring will keep.

A little leverage.

You are a spring leg, a flywheel, and a decidedly useful hook. Reach the striped gate. No lives, no damage, no timer limit. Every pennant is a checkpoint; the low floors are there to catch you.

Start with a hop

Point below and a little behind the machine, then hold left mouse to extend. Release to settle back to the normal length. Right mouse retracts the leg; tuck, then extend for a bigger hop. Aim at the ground you want to push away from, not the place you want to go.

Free aim. Real recoil.

The leg attaches at the central axle and can aim all the way around. A motor moves its resting angle, rather than teleporting the foot. The body takes the opposite reaction. In open air you can rearrange yourself, but you cannot steer the combined centre of mass.

Use your head. It is a hook.

Turn with A / D to put the open throat around a ledge or brass lip. The curved chassis can bear your weight and pivot on contact. There is no grab button, snap, glue, or invisible rope. Peel out with A, or push away with the leg to release. A deeply loaded hook may need a swing first. The brass practice rail is a good place to learn.

The flywheel is not a jetpack

A / D ask the motor for a steady turn rate, trading angular momentum between the wheel and chassis. Let go to coast against air resistance: slow turns remain useful, while fast spins fade quickly. Drag acts only on the exposed chassis, not the enclosed flywheel. The unequal coloured sectors and white spoke show its actual rotation. The two rim arcs and reserve bars show how much acceleration remains in each direction. At the wheel's speed limit that direction runs out. Reverse to recover headroom, or use S to brake the wheel relative to the chassis—which also reacts on the chassis. Air carries away chassis angular momentum; the flywheel does not magically recharge.
